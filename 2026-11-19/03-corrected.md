## Question 1: The Paradox of Dollar Hegemony

Although early cryptocurrency advocates envisioned a monetary system free from state control, privately issued stablecoins such as USDT and USDC have paradoxically reinforced the global dominance of the U.S. dollar. Because major issuers back their tokens one-to-one with conventional reserves—primarily short-term U.S. Treasury bills—they have become major private holders of American government debt while extending the dollar’s reach across global blockchains. At the same time, regulatory frameworks such as Europe’s MiCA and proposed U.S. federal legislation seek to impose strict reserve audits rather than ban these assets, gradually integrating digital dollars into the mainstream financial architecture as a regulated foundation for cashless payments.

## Question 2: The Central Bank Dilemma

The rise of dollar-pegged stablecoins undermines monetary sovereignty in emerging economies because anyone with a smartphone and an internet connection can operate twenty-four hours a day without seeking permission from a local commercial bank. In effect, digital dollars democratize currency substitution, allowing citizens to hedge against inflation and domestic monetary mismanagement while rendering traditional capital controls porous. Meanwhile, regulators in developed economies warn of systemic risks, noting that because stablecoins operate outside conventional deposit insurance and lender-of-last-resort protections, a sudden loss of confidence could trigger a digital bank run and force rapid liquidations of U.S. Treasury securities.

## Written production

The modern economy has undergone significant changes in recent years regarding traditional ways of storing and trading assets. Governments and institutional banks have encountered an unexpected rival in virtual assets—led by digital dollars and open markets—where no strict policies initially governed how people transacted with their money.

On the one hand, individual financial autonomy allows citizens to preserve their purchasing power against chronic inflation, currency devaluation, and rigid banking policies. Anyone with a basic mobile phone and an internet connection can manage digital dollars or cryptocurrencies from home. This innovative shift toward cashless finance has facilitated modern commerce and enabled ordinary individuals to trade and invest in tokens globally. At the outset, in an largely unregulated environment, users could buy and sell goods across borders without government surveillance, resembling the ultimate expression of a free market.

On the other hand, local central banks recognized that unchecked financial freedom weakened their monetary authority and could ultimately breed distrust, instability, and widespread losses from hacks, since no regulatory safety net protects tokens held in self-custody wallets. Slowly but relentlessly, governments have introduced regulations to integrate this cashless ecosystem into the conventional financial architecture; today, making digital payments appears far safer under statutory frameworks such as MiCA in Europe.

In conclusion, this tension between decentralized autonomy and institutional control highlights a fundamental economic trade-off. Mechanisms that bypassed traditional banking and enabled individuals to act as their own bank generated profound anxiety among state institutions—illustrated by early cases like Silk Road in the 2010s, where Ross Ulbricht built an anonymous marketplace powered by cryptocurrency. Examples like that demonstrate why governments worked tirelessly to prevent unregulated financial networks from spreading unchecked. Ultimately, policymakers realized that eliminating digital assets entirely was impossible; instead, their most viable strategy has been to absorb them gradually, allowing citizens to hold virtual dollars while maintaining underlying surveillance over transactions.
