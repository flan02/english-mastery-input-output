## Question 1: From Efficiency to Resilience

Multinational corporations have reshaped their trade models after realizing that concentrating production in a single distant hub created acute single-point vulnerabilities. To address these risks, they have increasingly prioritized supply chain diversification, which means relocating factories closer to end consumers or to countries aligned with their regional trade blocs. However, when emerging manufacturing hubs attract new investment, significant infrastructure challenges arise. Specifically, host nations must invest heavily in reliable energy grids, modernize industrial facilities, and streamline customs clearance procedures to prevent bottlenecks as export volumes grow.

## Question 2: Tariffs, Industrial Policy, and Digital Trade

Proponents of industrial policy argue that targeted regulations and tariffs are necessary to safeguard domestic manufacturing jobs, reduce strategic dependence on foreign rivals, and counteract state-subsidized overcapacity abroad. Conversely, trade economists criticize these measures because escalating tariff barriers risk fragmenting the global economy into competing regional blocs and passing higher prices on to consumers. Meanwhile, digitally delivered services such as software and cloud computing have expanded rapidly, enabling smaller enterprises to export high-value offerings globally with minimal overhead. Nevertheless, this digital growth introduces its own regulatory hurdles, including data restrictions and digital service taxes that complicate compliance for international firms.


## Written production

In this essay, I will examine how trade policies and government ideology can either foster or hinder domestic manufacturing across different economies.

Right-wing administrations typically advocate developing the economy through free markets, seeking to eliminate as many regulations, tariffs, and taxes as possible. From this perspective, policymakers should not interfere in private commercial agreements, as excessive state intervention creates market distortions and ultimately harms everyone participating in trade, whether small businesses or major corporations.

Conversely, left-leaning administrations argue that allowing large-scale enterprises to operate unchecked only widens the inequality gap among actors in the trade ecosystem. Their core strategy relies on active state intervention—deciding what constitutes fair trade and imposing strict regulations to level the playing field between major corporations and smaller participants.

Having outlined both models and their distinct winners, it is worth examining how small and medium-sized enterprises—analogous to "retail" participants in financial markets—can survive in an increasingly volatile global trade environment. Much like market "whales," large corporations possess the capital, networks, and resources to remain resilient regardless of the prevailing political ideology. By contrast, small businesses and everyday consumers are far more exposed to abrupt policy shifts.

In a free-market scenario characterized by nearshoring and the removal of protective tariffs, domestic manufacturers receive neither state subsidies nor shelter from foreign competitors. To survive, smaller firms must capitalize on deregulation by adopting an outward-looking mindset and seeking opportunities in accessible regional trade blocs. On the other hand, a market bloated with protective tariffs and rigid regulations can also prove detrimental to smaller players, as elevated input costs distort prices and weaken domestic consumption—potentially triggering high inflation if the government attempts to offset declining purchasing power through excessive money issuance and subsidies.
