## Question 1: Origins and Dual Structure of the Feudal World

The ninth-century crisis was triggered by the death of Charlemagne, which led to the fracture of the Carolingian Empire and left Western Europe vulnerable to both political fragmentation and external invasions. Out of this vacuum of sovereign authority emerged feudalism, a system built on exchanging land for military service and loyalty. On a political and military level, monarchs granted large tracts of territory—known as fiefs—to their powerful vassals, who knelt before their lord during a formal ceremony of homage to swear an oath of fealty. Conversely, the economic engine of this society was the manorial system, which organized rural life and agricultural production on each individual estate. The vast majority of the population consisted of serfs, who owed the lord several days of unpaid labor each week, a steep portion of their harvest, and usage fees. In exchange, serfs were bound to the soil for life; moreover, they were guaranteed physical protection behind the castle walls during raids.

## Question 2: Internal Conflicts and the Decline of Feudalism

Medieval Europe was chronically plagued by violent jurisdictional disputes and internal warfare because vassals frequently held fiefs from multiple lords simultaneously—a tangled arrangement known as subinfeudation. Furthermore, ambitious barons waged private wars over disputed borders, devastating peasant crops in the process. To contain this bloodshed, the Catholic Church intervened through movements such as the Peace of God and the Truce of God, restricting fighting on religious holidays and protecting non-combatants. Over time, however, a flourishing economy during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries brought improved agricultural techniques, which allowed merchants to accumulate liquid capital and enabled kings to bypass feudal levies. Finally, the demographic catastrophe of the Black Death in the mid-fourteenth century decimated Europe’s labor force, giving surviving peasants unprecedented bargaining power to demand cash wages and personal freedom.

## Written production

The following essay compares the strengths, drawbacks, and trade-offs of highly decentralized power structures versus strong centralized governance.

During the feudal era, the absence of a centralized bureaucracy and enforceable laws enabled a new system to emerge. Power was divided into small units and distributed across individual fiefs. Local lords dictated the rules and guaranteed physical protection, food, and small plots of land in exchange for unpaid labor, military service, and loyalty from each serf.

The medieval era demonstrated that a highly decentralized structure allowed Western Europe to survive relentless invasions and warfare, yet it also bred chronic internal conflicts, power struggles, and betrayal. After the French Revolution, modern bureaucracy gradually replaced private wars, and democratic institutions allowed states to concentrate authority within a centralized governance model. This resolved several conflicts that feudalism could not handle, such as maintaining public order, peace, and civil rights—issues that the Catholic Church had tried to contain but could never fully eliminate.

Later on, globalization enabled people to communicate with each other across borders, merchants traded freely, and geopolitical dialogue became more decisive than the sword and shield. In this interconnected world, English became a versatile global lingua franca. By contrast, feudalism suffered from severe coordination failures due to unruly local rulers, arbitrary norms, and the absence of laws protecting individual rights. Over the years, these conditions generated widespread unhappiness among populations who saw violent conflict as their only means of survival.

As a final reflection, it is worth asking what would have happened if decentralized feudal power had not been disrupted by the Black Death and peasants had not gained unprecedented bargaining power from acute labor shortages. Would feudalism have collapsed anyway, or could it have persisted into the twentieth century? Had society remained trapped in a fragmented system driven by warfare, submission, and widespread illiteracy, modern pillars such as democracy, global trade, electricity, universities, the internet, and artificial intelligence might never have existed. Today, feudalism looks like a medieval dictatorship, yet it may have been the only viable survival mechanism given the scarce resources of that era.
