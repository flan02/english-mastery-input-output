## Question 1: Structural Causes of the Two-Party System

The author compares European democracies—which operate under proportional representation—with the U.S. "winner-take-all" framework, where the candidate with a simple plurality wins the entire seat and leaves third parties with no viable path to power. Over the past 150 years, this structural reality has cemented a rigid two-party duopoly that shapes not only legislative debates in Congress, but also cultural identity at the dinner table. Furthermore, the author explains that the ideological overlap that once allowed lawmakers to cross the aisle and strike bipartisan compromises on infrastructure, civil rights, and budgets gradually evaporated over the last three decades, giving way to deep "partisan sorting" and affective polarization.

## Question 2: Independents, Swing States, and Legislative Gridlock

Although more than forty percent of Americans now self-identify as independents—outnumbering both registered Democrats and Republicans—political scientists point out a crucial caveat: most of them are actually "partisan leaners" who consistently vote for one party over the other. Because partisans are geographically sorted, elections hinge on turnout and a tiny fraction of truly undecided voters in key "swing states" via the Electoral College. Meanwhile, once a new president is elected, governing remains remarkably difficult due to institutional hurdles like the Senate filibuster, forcing executives to rely on unilateral executive orders that the next administration can easily overturn with the stroke of a pen.

## Written production

As a political researcher, I propose three structural reforms to modernize our democratic system and enable citizens to make the best possible choice in every election.   First, we should build transparent digital platforms capable of tracking voter sentiment in real time across every state. This tool would allow citizens to anticipate potential electoral scenarios ahead of Election Day by analyzing aggregated, anonymized engagement trends across social media posts, articles, and public discourse on mobile and desktop devices.   Second, I propose introducing a secure, private digital voting infrastructure audited in real time by a bipartisan oversight board with representatives from each party. This reform would strengthen transparency during the voting process and dismantle entrenched political machines that exploit vulnerabilities on Election Day.   Last but not least, I would introduce a draft bill prohibiting both chambers of Congress from shelving or dismissing incoming legislation before formally debating it for at least one year. This mandate would prevent politicians from paralyzing our democracy through procedural gridlock and ensure a fairer path forward for major bipartisan bills.   Over the past twenty years, the internet and the rise of social media have empowered everyday citizens to build tangible solutions and hold institutions visible and accountable. Therefore, we must invest effort into creating transparent civic tools that help our society function at its highest potential. Building a direct bridge that connects constituents with their representatives in every state should be mandatory to reduce blind partisan leaning and prioritize clear ideological principles over short-term personal interests, which are far harder to measure. Ultimately, consistent principles define who we are and act as a compass throughout our lives—because while core beliefs remind you of who you are, shifting self-interests will eventually change who you are.
