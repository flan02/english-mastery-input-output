## Question 1: Maximizers vs. Satisficers

The text presents a clear distinction between two cognitive decision-making styles. The first group consists of **maximizers**, who—according to the author—struggle to make quick decisions, take their time evaluating every possible option, and experience mental fatigue rapidly due to the sheer number of alternatives they weigh before committing to a choice. Furthermore, the author notes that this group ends up significantly less happy than the other because overanalyzing options triggers analysis paralysis and post-decision regret. On the other hand, **satisficers** pick wisely yet much faster than maximizers; by relying on clear baseline standards, they avoid the pressure of comparing endless items, which ultimately leads to a happier, more content life.

## Question 2: The Physiology of Decision Fatigue

The author explains that when decision fatigue sets in, the brain starts looking for mental shortcuts to conserve energy. This depletion generally manifests in one of two ways: reckless impulsivity or complete avoidance. A famous study examining judicial rulings in parole hearings demonstrated that judges were far more likely to grant parole early in the morning or immediately after a lunch break. As the day wore on, however, their mentally fatigued brains defaulted to the safest, lowest-effort option: denying parole and maintaining the status quo.
