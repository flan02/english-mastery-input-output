According to the author, the Victorian way of life presented several controversies compared to modern societies. Since they did not exercise power strictly through laws and formal institutions—creating what appeared to be a power vacuum—their system required a high level of trust, where one's last name was more important than skills or merits. The author explains that the boundary between work and social life barely existed; therefore, we can assume from his words that success and effectiveness stemmed from a system where confidence and mutual recognition mattered more than the work itself.

## Question 2: Social Cohesion vs. Specialization

Regarding the Victorian British educational system, they did not rely on specialization; instead, they taught every child the same foundation in classical education. The author mentions that this served a hidden organizational purpose. Rather than specializing students in a specific area, schools gave them a common vocabulary, a set of historical analogies, and a unified moral framework. As a result of those teachings, when two officials educated at Oxford had to communicate with each other, they already shared the same cultural shorthand.
